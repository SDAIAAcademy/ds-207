The idea of this session

An aggregate table can point at one student in three ways

A small cell

A count of 1 in "Computing, non-Saudi, registered disability" is a person, to anyone who knows that college.

A total

Hide the 1 and print the row total: the reader subtracts and has it back.

A second table

Two tables that each look safe differ by exactly the students one of them leaves out.

Singling out means a reader can tell that a cell is one particular person, and so learns what the table says about that person.

The three ways are the three attacks of Lab 2.1. Ask who the attacker is: not a hacker, a classmate, a lecturer, a journalist with the earlier report. The US Department of Education's guidance uses a "reasonable person in the school community" as the standard.

Sources: Privacy Technical Assistance Center, Frequently Asked Questions: Disclosure Avoidance.

The rule

A threshold rule: no count from 1 to 9 leaves the unit

  • The unit's release policy sets 10. It is the University's own choice
  • No national instrument sets a number. The national guideline requires that aggregated data cannot be used to identify a person
  • Practice elsewhere: 3 as an absolute floor, 5 or 10 as common minimums

Choosing a threshold

Higher when the attribute is sensitive (health), when the population is small and people know each other (a college), and when more tables on the same population will follow.

Be exact about what is law and what is house policy: the PDPL and its guideline give the goal, the number is the unit's. The 3, 5 and 10 come from the US Department of Education's guidance for education data and are cited as practice, not as a Saudi rule. The policy text is labs/data/release_policy.md.

Sources: Personal Data Destruction, Anonymization and Pseudonymisation Guideline; Privacy Technical Assistance Center, Frequently Asked Questions: Disclosure Avoidance.

Beyond suppression

Suppression is one of four tools

MethodWhat it doesWhat the reader loses
Coarsen the categoriesMerge colleges, or nationalities into two groupsDetail; usually the cheapest loss
SuppressHide small cells and their complementsCells, sometimes most of them
Round or bandCounts to a base of 5; "fewer than 10"Exactness of every cell
PerturbSmall random changes to countsTrust that a number is the number
Later. Noise with a mathematical guarantee (differential privacy) and measured re-identification risk are in DS-309.

The lab's stretch is rounding. For public accountability tables, perturbation is often judged unsuitable because readers can no longer trust a figure. Whatever the method, published exact totals reopen the subtraction attack.

Sources: Privacy Technical Assistance Center, Frequently Asked Questions: Disclosure Avoidance (suppression, blurring, perturbation).

Not only small counts

A cell of zero or of everyone can disclose too

Everyone in a group

"All 14 students of programme X were referred." No small cell, and you now know the referral status of each of the 14.

Nobody in a group

"0 of 14 were not referred" is the same sentence. A zero that tells you about everyone else is not harmless.

  • Check rates of 0% and 100% in small groups as well as counts
  • The unit's rule covers counts of 1 to 9; this case needs your judgement and a line in the log

This is group disclosure: nobody is singled out, yet everyone in the group is described. The illustration of 14 students is an example, not a number from the data. Ask where it could occur in the Awn tables: a small programme where every student was referred.

Sources: Privacy Technical Assistance Center, Frequently Asked Questions: Disclosure Avoidance.

A working definition

Bias is a systematic error that falls on some groups more than others

  • It needs no intent: a process that records or covers people unequally is enough
  • It is not noise: more data of the same kind does not remove it
  • It enters before any metric is computed

The national frame

Fairness is the first of SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles: act to eliminate bias in the data, the design and the use of a system. The AI Bias Reference Guide names more than a hundred types, with how each arises and how to mitigate it.

Awn is a two-line rule, not a trained model. Everything the guide says about data applies to it all the same: the rule's inputs are recorded data and its thresholds were set on historical data.

Sources: AI Ethics Principles, principle 1 (fairness); AI Bias Reference Guide, introduction.

Entry points 5 and 6

What happens after the referral changes what the data says about it

  • Support that works hides itself. A referred student who is helped and passes counts as a referral that was "not needed"
  • Take-up differs. Who answers an advisor's call, who can come during working hours?
  • One number for everyone hides a rule that fails a small group

The loop

If next year's thresholds are tuned on this year's outcomes, colleges with good advisors will look as if they need fewer referrals. The guide's feedback loop bias.

The unit cannot separate "false alarm" from "helped" without a comparison group, and it has none. That sentence goes into the limits statement of Lab 3.4. Combining groups that differ so that the result fits the largest one is the guide's aggregation bias.

Sources: AI Bias Reference Guide, feedback loop bias and aggregation bias.

Method

An entry point is a hypothesis until someone measures it

An opinion

"Attendance data is probably unfair to some students." Nobody can act on it, and nobody can refute it.

A measurement

"For 300 students with a remote accommodation the record is 17 points below the roll call; 191 fall under the limit on paper, 14 in fact." A size, a group, a consequence.

  • The audit sampled all 300 students of the small group and 1,300 others at random: a small group needs all its members to be measured at all

The design point matters for participants' own work: a simple random sample of 1,600 from 18,000 would have held about 27 students with a remote accommodation. Sampling the small group in full is what makes the comparison possible.

Why two error rates

The two errors hurt different students in different ways

A false referral

A student who was fine is contacted, labelled "at risk" in a system, and takes an advisor's hour from someone else. Mild once; not mild if the label travels.

A miss

A student who is struggling hears from nobody. The rule existed for this student.

  • A group can be worse off in one error and not the other, so one combined number hides it
  • The ethics principles ask that metrics be chosen by the effect of the decision and the harm of each kind of error

The two gaps found in the lab are in different errors: false referrals for one group, misses for another. A single fairness score would have averaged them.

Sources: AI Ethics Principles, principle 1 (fairness assessment and choice of metrics).

The limit of the numbers

A fairness metric cannot tell you which fairness to want

  • When groups differ in how often the outcome occurs, a rule cannot equalise every error rate and every hit rate at once
  • Lowering false referrals for a group raises its misses, or the reverse
  • How large a remaining gap is acceptable is not a statistical question

Who decides

The director of the unit owns the rule; the data protection officer owns the impact on students. You bring the rates, the intervals, the cause you could establish and what stays open. They choose.

The impossibility results are for risk scores and classifiers; the trade between the two errors applies to any rule with a threshold. In the case the outcome rates do differ a little: 14.2% of students with a registered disability struggled against 12.1% of others. The lab's stretch makes the trade visible.

Sources: Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores; Chouldechova, Fair prediction with disparate impact.
