Review of Day 2

What is the right next step?

A rule misses struggling non-Saudi students more often than Saudi students: 62% against 43%, and the interval of the difference excludes zero. What does the analyst do next?

Propose a lower threshold for non-Saudi students
Report that the rule discriminates by nationality
Name two candidate causes and run the check on which they disagree
Leave the breakdown by nationality out of the report
Why. A gap is an observation. Yesterday's check showed the cause was missing marks for bridging-year entrants, and the fix was a data feed.

Answer: the third option. Recall the result: with a mark present both groups are missed at similar rates; with the mark missing both are missed three times in four or more.

Honesty in the datasheet

A datasheet that states the data's weakness protects the analysis

Collection, as written in the lab

"Known to be recorded badly: attendance is about 17 points too low for the 300 students with a remote accommodation, because remote sessions leave no card reading."

Composition, as written in the lab

"The midterm mark is missing for 1,244 students, all bridging-year entrants."

  • A reader who finds the weakness alone stops trusting everything
  • A reader who is told can weigh the results that depend on it

Both sentences are from the solution of Lab 3.1 and both numbers from Day 2. Transparency is one of the national AI ethics principles; for an analyst it starts here, with saying what is wrong with your own data.

Sources: AI Ethics Principles (transparency and explainability).

The analysis log

Every decision has a reason, a date and an approver

IdDecisionApprover
L001Evaluate Awn on a pseudonymised extract of 13 fieldsRegistrar (data owner)
L002Add four group fields for the comparisonData protection officer
L003Release two one-way tables, not the cross tableRegistrar; suppression checked by the director
L004Do not release the Saudi-only table by collegeRegistrar
L005Report bridging-year entrants and remote accommodation separatelyDirector of the unit
Six fields per entry. Id, date, decision, reason, approver, where the evidence is.

The first two entries are supplied; the lab adds three from Day 2. A log is written when the decision is made, not reconstructed at the end: the date is part of the evidence.

Accountability

An approver is a named person with the authority to approve that thing

The data owner

Approves extracts and releases of the data he is responsible for. Here: the registrar.

The data protection officer

Decides on sensitive fields, new purposes, disclosures and transfers.

The owner of the decision

Approves how results are reported and used. Here: the director of the unit.

What goes wrong. "Approved by the team." A team cannot be asked a question in an audit, and cannot be shown to have had the authority.

The analyst's own name appears as the author of each entry, never as the approver of her own release. The second-person check on suppression from Day 2 is the same idea at small scale.

Sources: AI Ethics Principles (accountability and responsibility).

Decision records and versioned releases

A release is a version, a date, fingerprints and one line of warning

{"version": "2026.11.1",
 "table": "Referral rate by college",
 "files": {"analysis_extract.csv":
           "2d9ebc9f490e9f0d"},
 "log_entries": ["L001", "L002", "L003"],
 "approved_by": "the registrar",
 "must_not_be_used_for":
   "ranking colleges or judging advisors"}
  • A fingerprint (a hash of the file) changes if one byte changes: a version number is a claim, a fingerprint is a check
  • A decision record keeps the options that were rejected, and why
  • Templates live in Git beside the code, as Markdown or Quarto documents

The fingerprint shown is the one the lab computes for the course's extract. The lab's stretch is a decision record for the threshold of 10: context, options (3, 5, 10, rounding), decision, who, what would reopen it. The register of releases is also what makes Day 2's differencing check possible.

Not only good practice

Some of this record is required

  • The University keeps a record of processing: the purpose, the categories of people, who the data is disclosed to, transfers, the retention period
  • For statistics without consent, the purpose is stated in that record
  • Sharing requests and their documents are kept for five years
  • Disposal of data is logged

Your log feeds theirs

The data protection officer keeps the record of processing. An analysis log with purposes, approvals and end dates is what lets her keep it true.

Tie back to Day 1: the duties of the statistics article included stating the purpose in the record. The analyst does not write the University's record; she supplies what it needs.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Article 31; Implementing Regulation, Article 30; Data Sharing Policy, clause Eighth (paragraph 7); Data Classification Policy, disposal controls.

The peer audit

Five questions your documents must answer alone

  1. Which file and which version produced the referral table released in November?
  2. Who approved the use of disability status in the group comparison, and on what condition?
  3. Why are bridging-year entrants reported separately?
  4. What must the group comparison not be used for?
  5. How many cells were suppressed in the release, and who checked the suppression?
The rule of the swap. Your peer reads only your documents. No asking, no notebook.

These are the questions of labs/data/audit_questions.json. Each maps to one place: the release record, log entry L002, log entry L005, the datasheet's "uses", log entry L003. "Cannot answer" is the useful result: it shows which sentence is missing.

Checkpoint

Which log entry would survive an audit?

The analyst decided to merge two small nationality groups in a published table. Which entry lets a reviewer trace that decision?

"2026-11-09. Merged groups X and Y in table T1: both had cells under 10. Approved by the registrar (data owner). Evidence: suppression log."
"Merged some categories in T1 for privacy reasons. Approved."
A comment in the notebook cell that does the merge
"2026-11-09. Merged X and Y in T1. Decided by the analyst."
Why. Date, decision, reason, a named role with the authority, and where the evidence is. Remove any one and a question in the audit has no answer.

Answer: the first option. The fourth option has a date and a decision and still fails: nobody with authority over the data agreed.

Session 2

Scoping with a sponsor

  • Ask the questions that turn a request into a decision, a population, a measure and a time frame
  • Agree success criteria and what is out of scope before any analysis starts
  • Record the agreement in a scope note the sponsor confirms
02
How requests arrive

A request is usually one sentence, and the sentence is not the question

DirectorIs Awn working? The vice-rector will ask me next month.
Analyst, too fastI will send you the referral numbers by Thursday.
AnalystWhat will you decide once you know?
  • "Working" could mean reaching struggling students, helping them, or costing less
  • Each meaning needs different data, and one of them cannot be answered at all
  • Starting the analysis before asking is the most expensive way to find that out

The exchange is brief B2 of the lab. The question that cannot be answered is whether support helps: there is no comparison group. Better to discover that in a six-minute conversation than in the fourth week.

The conversation

Four questions turn a request into something answerable

The decision

"What will you decide once you know?"

The population

"Which students, which term?"

The measure

"What would you count, and compared with what?"

The time

"When is the decision taken?"

Order matters. The decision first. It tells you which answer would change what the sponsor does, and it is the purpose the law asks you for.

The same four parts as the purpose sentence of Day 1: a well-scoped question and a lawful purpose are the same sentence. Model the first question aloud in different words; participants need phrases that fit their own voice.

Success criteria

Agree what a useful answer looks like before you produce one

  • "If the answer were X, what would you do? And if it were Y?"
  • The form: one page, a table, a number with its definition
  • The precision that matters: to the point, or "more than half"?
  • What would make the sponsor not believe the answer

In brief B2

"One page the director can take to the council: the two rates, the groups for which they differ clearly, and the cause where it could be established."

If no possible answer would change the decision, the analysis is not needed, and saying so is a service. The criterion on the card is from the solution's scope note.

Out of scope

Write down what you will not do, while everyone still agrees

The sponsor's opening sentenceWhat was really neededOut of scope
"I need the data on why engineering students are failing."Whether to add tutorial sections to two first-year coursesNaming students or instructors
"Is Awn working?"Extend the rule, or change its thresholds firstWhether support helps: no comparison group
"A nice number for the annual report."One figure with its definition, beside last year'sBreakdowns with small counts
Requests grow. The line "no lists of students" is the one you will point to in three weeks.

Technique

Three habits make the six minutes work

Ask, then wait

One question at a time. The pause after it is where the real answer comes.

Say it back

"So the decision is whether to add tutorials, and you need it in three weeks." Sponsors correct a summary more easily than they answer a question.

Propose the question

Leave with a sentence that ends in a question mark and that data can answer. Read it aloud before you go.

Common failure in the role-play: the analyst explains methods. A sponsor has no opinion on Wilson intervals and a strong one on deadlines. Second failure: accepting "everything you have" as an answer; the next question is "what will you decide with it, and by when?"

Role-play · three rounds

Six minutes a round: four to talk, two for feedback. Rotate the roles each round

One round

  • Round 1 · brief B1
  • Round 2 · brief B2
  • Round 3 · brief B3

Come back to this slide during Lab 3.2 for the three rounds; reset the timer between rounds. The sponsor shows the card only in the feedback. Call "feedback" at four minutes. The observer speaks first: what was found out, what was not, the best question heard. Then the sponsor shows the card.

Checkpoint

Which is the best first question?

A dean says: "Get me the data on why our students are failing." What does the analyst ask first?

"Which fields do you want in the extract?"
"Would you prefer a regression or a simple breakdown?"
"Do you mean first-year students or all students?"
"What do you have to decide, and by when?"
Why. The decision tells you which answer would change what the dean does, what data that needs and what is out of scope. Every other question depends on it.

Answer: the fourth option. The third option is a good question in the wrong place; ask the room what could go wrong if it came first. The analyst might scope a first-year study for a decision about final-year placements.

Session 3

Pushing back without obstructing

  • Refuse an unsafe request by naming the harm and the rule, not by citing policy at the sponsor
  • Offer a lawful alternative that serves the sponsor's decision
  • Recognise when to escalate to the data protection officer and record the agreement
03
The request

"A list of every student likely to fail, with names and a score, by Sunday"

Why it is unsafe

A named list of predicted failure is a new purpose and reaches people who do not advise the student. The score is wrong for known groups. A label that travels to deans can follow a student into grading and discipline.

What she has to decide

Where to put twelve new advisors, and whether the colleges act on referrals. Neither decision needs a name.

The sponsor is not the villain. Dr. Ibtisam Al-Khayyat wants struggling students helped. The request is the wrong tool for a good aim.

The request is in labs/data/risk_list_request.json. Set the tone before the role-play: analysts who treat the sponsor as an opponent lose the conversation and the relationship. The harm to name is the third one, in plain words.

The structure

A refusal that works has five parts, and the alternative comes early

1 · Listenwhat must she decide?
→
2 · Alternativewhat serves that decision
→
3 · Harmto students, in plain words
→
4 · Ruleand who decides
→
5 · Recordwhat was agreed
Why this order. She came with a problem. Someone who hears first how it gets solved by Sunday can listen to the reason. Someone who hears a rule first hears a refusal.

These five are the observer's rubric in the lab, scored 0 to 2 each. The order of 2 and 3 can swap; what must not happen is rule first, alternative never. "Policy says no" ends the conversation and sends the sponsor to someone less careful.

What you will hear

Three pressures, and a reply to each that is not a rule

She saysA reply that keeps the conversation
"The council meets on Monday.""You will have the table for the council on Sunday. It answers where the advisors go."
"Another university does this already.""Ours would wrongly list most students who attend remotely with an accommodation. I would not want your name on that list."
"I am the vice-rector. I am allowed to see student data.""You are. The question is what this decision needs, and it does not need names. If you need them, the data protection officer decides, and I will take it to her today."

The third reply does three things: concedes what is true, returns to the decision, and names who decides. Access follows the purpose and the need to know, not rank. The claim in the second reply is Day 2's finding: 59% false referrals for that group.

Escalation

Escalating is saying aloud whose decision it is

  • When: the sponsor still wants named or sensitive data, a new purpose, a disclosure or a transfer
  • To whom: the data protection officer, who monitors how the law is applied and supervises impact assessments
  • How: three lines: what was asked and by whom, what you offered, the decision you need

Copy the sponsor

Escalation said aloud and copied is a service: the decision was never the analyst's, and never the sponsor's alone. Escalation behind someone's back is a complaint.

The stretch of the lab is the three-line message. The analyst does not refuse an instruction on her own authority and does not carry it out on her own authority either: she routes it.

Sources: Implementing Regulation, Article 32 (data protection officer).

The other failure

Obstructing is as much a failure as complying

Comply

Send the list. The harm happens, with your name in the log.

Obstruct

"We cannot, it is the law." No alternative, no route. The sponsor asks someone else, who sends a worse list.

Push back

The decision is served by Sunday, the names stay with advisors, the record says who agreed, and the open question has an owner.

The record. Asked for, will deliver, will not deliver, why, who decides more, confirmed by. What is not written down was not agreed.

The six fields are the agreement of task 5. Ask the room which failure is more common where they work. In most organisations it is the second, and it is the one nobody is blamed for.

Checkpoint

Which reply pushes back without obstructing?

A dean asks for the names of students who used the counselling service, "so lecturers can go easy on them". Which reply is best?

"I cannot. Counselling data is health data."
"You want lecturers to support students under strain. I can give counts by department, and the service can contact lecturers for students who agree. Names would break what students were told."
"I will send the list marked confidential, for department heads only."
"That is a question for the data protection officer, not for me."
Why. It names the dean's aim, offers two ways to serve it, and gives the reason in terms of students.

Answer: the second option. The first is true and useless; the fourth is correct about who decides and skips everything the analyst can do before that. Health data: the fewest staff who need it to provide the service.

Session 4

Explaining limits

  • State what an analysis cannot show, separately from how uncertain it is
  • Write a limits statement a non-specialist cannot misread
  • Choose the capstone engagement and list what its pack must contain
04
Two different things

An interval tells you how sure; a limit tells you what was never measured

Uncertainty

How far the number could move if you measured again. Awn referred 55% of struggling students, between 53% and 57%. Small, with 2,191 students.

Limits

What the number does not measure however often you measure: whether support helps, whether the label is right, whether the record is right, whether next term will be like this one.

What goes wrong. A tight interval read as "the analysis is solid". Precision about the wrong thing is still the wrong thing.

The interval is the lab's first task: 1,209 of 2,191. Most misuse of analytical results comes from limits, not from uncertainty. The limits of the Awn evaluation were found on Day 2 and written into the datasheet this morning; now they go next to the number.

The Awn evaluation

Six real limits, and two that only sound like limits

  • No test of whether support helps: no comparison group
  • One term of one year
  • Attendance under-recorded for remote accommodation
  • Marks missing for bridging-year entrants
  • "Struggled" is one definition; withdrawals can count as "ok"
  • It describes groups, not any one student's risk

Not limits

"The sample is too small": 18,000 students give the overall rate to about two points. "The data is pseudonymised": a pseudonym changes who can be identified, not any rate.

Task 2 of Lab 3.4. False modesty is a failure too: a list of limits padded with things that are not limits teaches the reader to skip the list.

The limits statement

Three sentences that travel with the number

  1. What it shows. "In term 1, Awn referred 55% of the students who ended the term struggling (between 53% and 57%), so it missed 45% of them."
  2. What it does not show, and for whom it is worse. "The analysis did not test whether the support that follows a referral helps, and the rule is known to be less reliable for two groups: it wrongly refers many students with a remote-attendance accommodation and misses most struggling bridging-year entrants."
  3. What it must not be used for. "The result describes groups of students and must not be used to judge, rank or list any individual student."

The solution's statement: three sentences, 83 words. It goes in the report, on the slide and in the email, every time the number goes. For a slide it shortens to "missed 45%; unreliable for two groups; not for judging individuals".

Testing the statement

A statement works if it blocks the ways a hurried reader will misread the number

The misreadingBlocked by
"Awn causes students to pass."Sentence 2: support was not tested
"A student Awn did not refer is safe."Sentence 1: it missed 45%
"The rule works equally well for every group."Sentence 2: the two groups, named
"The list of referred students can decide who repeats a course."Sentence 3: must not be used for
Which side of the number. "Referred 55%" invites the second misreading. "Missed 45%" blocks it. Same fact.

Task 4 of the lab. Writing the misreadings down first is the method: ask "how will the most hurried reader use this?" and write against that. Explaining limits to a non-specialist is one of the three conversations the course practises, and the only one that is usually in writing.

Who acts on the result

A referral is a prompt for a person, and the statement keeps it that way

  • Awn's output goes to an advisor, who talks to the student and decides
  • People tend to favour a system's output over other evidence: the guide's automation bias
  • "Must not be used to judge, rank or list" is the line that protects the advisor's judgement

In every release

The unit's policy asks for a version, a date, an approver and a line that says what the table must not be used for. The line is not decoration: it is the record that you said it.

Human oversight is among the national AI ethics principles; a two-line rule needs it as much as a model does. If a referral list is later used for grading or discipline, the release record shows the unit said it must not be.

Sources: AI Ethics Principles (humanity; accountability and responsibility); AI Bias Reference Guide, automation bias.

Checkpoint

Which sentence belongs under the number?

A slide for the University council will say "Awn referred 55% of struggling students". There is room for one more line. Which one?

"95% confidence interval: 53% to 57%."
"Computed on a pseudonymised extract of 18,000 students."
"It missed 45%, is unreliable for two groups, and must not be used to judge individual students."
"Results should be interpreted with caution."
Why. The line that travels with a number should block its likeliest misuse. Here that is treating the rule as complete, as equal for all, and as a verdict on a student.

Answer: the third option. The first is correct statistics and the wrong priority. The fourth is the sentence everyone has written; ask what a reader does differently after reading it. Nothing.
