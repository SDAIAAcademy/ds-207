The first idea

Every step of an analysis is "processing" in the law

  • The PDPL's definition lists collecting, storing, linking, using, disclosing, sharing, publishing and destroying
  • A query, a join, a chart and an email attachment are each on that list
  • The University is the controller: it sets the purpose. You act for it
  • So the question is never "is this analysis?" but "which rules does this step engage?"

At Al-Qimam

Pulling 30 columns "to have a look" is collecting and storing. Joining the counselling register is linking. Sending a table to a dean is disclosing inside the controller; sending it to the Observatory is disclosing outside it.

Read the definition of processing in Article 1 aloud once. The point is that there is no neutral, pre-legal phase called "exploration".

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Article 1.

Lawful basis

A university's analysis rarely rests on consent

  • Consent is the default, and Article 6 lists cases that need none
  • An agreement the student is party to, or another law: enrolment, teaching, assessment, support
  • Legitimate interest of the controller: never for sensitive data
  • Advertising sent to a person needs prior consent, whatever else is true
What goes wrong. "They agreed to the terms at registration" used as a basis for anything. The basis covers the purposes in the notice. A new purpose needs its own gate.

Show the privacy notice of the case (labs/data/privacy_notice.md): teaching, assessment, advice and support, stipends, legal reporting, and the sentence "does not use your data for advertising". Request R4 in the lab dies on that sentence and on Article 25.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Articles 5, 6, 25 and 26.

The analyst's own article

Statistics without consent are allowed, on four duties

The law allows it when

the data does not specifically identify the person, or the evidence of identity is destroyed before the data is disclosed (not for sensitive data), or another law or an agreement requires it.

  1. State the purpose in the record of processing
  2. Collect only the minimum the purpose needs
  3. Pseudonymise wherever the purpose can still be met
  4. Make sure the work has no negative impact on the people in the data

This is the article that makes most of the unit's work possible, and its duties are today's first two labs: minimum data and a pseudonym. Duty four is Day 2 and Day 3: a result that singles out a student, or a list that follows a student into grading, is a negative impact.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Article 27; Implementing Regulation, Article 30.

Sensitive data

Health data sits in three ordinary-looking columns

ColumnWhy it is health data

  • The PDPL's sensitive kinds include health data, ethnic origin, beliefs, criminal and biometric data
  • Health data: access for the fewest staff who need it to provide the service
  • Legitimate interest never covers it
  • A registered or none flag is still health data

Nationality is not on the PDPL's sensitive list by name; racial or ethnic origin is. Treat nationality with care in small groups all the same: Day 2 shows a table where nationality and disability together point at one student.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Articles 1, 6 and 23.

Rights and retention

A student can ask what you hold, and the extract has an end date

  • Rights: to be informed, to access, to get a copy, to correct, to have data destroyed when no longer needed
  • The University answers within 30 days
  • Retention: destroy personal data when the purpose is met; data that no longer identifies anyone may be kept

What this means at your desk

Know where every copy of an extract is. Give each extract a destruction date in the log. Keep the pseudonym key in the unit, so a student's rows can be found for an access request and nowhere else.

The stretch of Lab 1.1 is an access request from a student who heard about the Awn study. The answer goes through the data protection officer; the analyst's part is being able to find the rows.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Articles 4, 18 and 21; Implementing Regulation (time to respond).

When data leaves

Three words that are not synonyms: disclosure, transfer, breach

Disclosure

Someone outside the controller gets access. Allowed only in listed cases, such as a public entity asking for a public-interest purpose.

Transfer

The data is processed outside the Kingdom. Needs an adequate level of protection or approved safeguards, and the minimum data.

Breach

Access nobody allowed: a lost laptop, a file sent to the wrong list. The University notifies the authority within 72 hours where harm is expected.

Your part in a breach. Tell the data protection officer at once. The 72 hours run from when the University knows, and you are the University.

A free online notebook hosted abroad is a transfer even when nobody else ever opens the file. That is request R5 in the lab. Safeguards named in the transfer regulation include standard contractual clauses and binding common rules; an analyst does not arrange these alone.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Articles 15, 20 and 29; Regulation on Personal Data Transfer outside the Kingdom; Implementing Regulation (breach notification).

Who decides

The analyst prepares the decision; the data protection officer owns the hard ones

  • She monitors how the law is applied and supervises impact assessments
  • She receives data subjects' requests and reports breaches
  • She keeps the record of processing up to date

Rule of thumb

Inside the collection purpose: proceed, minimise, record. Anything else (a new purpose, sensitive data, a disclosure, a transfer, advertising): to her before any data is touched, with your note attached.

The note is the lab's last task: what was asked, the kind of processing, the rules engaged, what happens next. A data protection officer who receives that note can answer in minutes; one who receives "can I send this?" cannot.

Sources: Implementing Regulation, Article 32.

Purpose

A purpose is precise enough when it can reject a column

Too loose to decide anything

"Understand student success." Every one of the 30 columns might help with that.

Precise enough to cut with

"Evaluate whether Awn's referrals in term 1 reached the students who ended the term struggling, by college and level, to decide whether to extend the rule."

  • It names the decision, the population, the measure and the breakdown
  • The law: collect for purposes directly related to the controller's own, and process only for those

Have the room test the second sentence against three columns: home address (no), level (yes), nationality (no, not for this purpose). The same four parts return on Day 3 as the scope note.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Articles 10 and 11.

Minimisation

Every field gets one of three verdicts

Keep

The purpose needs it as it is: the rule's inputs, its output, the outcome, the breakdown.

Coarsen

The purpose needs a rougher form: a band for a date, a flag for a grade, a pseudonym for a student number.

Drop

The purpose does not need it. "It might be useful" is not a need.

The legal test. Content limited to the minimum necessary for the purpose, and nothing that identifies a person once the purpose is achieved.

Coarsening is the verdict people forget. Date of birth to birth year, exact grade to struggled or not, registry number to a project pseudonym. The national guideline names generalisation and aggregation as techniques.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Article 11; Personal Data Destruction, Anonymization and Pseudonymisation Guideline.

A word used loosely

"Anonymised" has a legal meaning, and your extract does not meet it

Pseudonymised

Identifiers replaced by a code. Someone holds the key, and each row is still one student. Still personal data. Every rule still applies.

Anonymised

Re-identification is impossible, the controller has assessed that, and keeps assessing it as methods change. Only then is it no longer personal data.

At your desk. Say "pseudonymised extract". Use a keyed hash with a key for this project only, so two extracts cannot be joined.

FN-106 attacked a pseudonymised file by linkage; do not repeat it, recall it. The measured version (k-anonymity, uniqueness) is in DS-309. Here the participant only needs the vocabulary to be exact, because the classification in session 3 depends on it.

Sources: Implementing Regulation, Article 9; Personal Data Destruction, Anonymization and Pseudonymisation Guideline.

Secondary use

A new purpose for old data is a new decision

  • Secondary use: using data for a purpose other than the one it was collected for
  • The PDPL allows it only in listed cases: consent, a public entity acting in the public interest, data that no longer identifies anyone, and a few more
  • The General Rules for Secondary Use of Data govern requests between entities for the public interest or for research

What the Rules ask of an applicant

A legitimate purpose that is not profit-seeking. Only the minimum data for it. A request to the entity that is the source of the data, or proof of its approval. A rejection must give its reasons.

The Rules complement the Data Sharing Policy; session 4 uses both. Inside the University the same idea holds without the Rules: the dean's request and the communications office's request are secondary uses, and neither fits a listed case.

Sources: Personal Data Protection Law, Article 10; General Rules for Secondary Use of Data.

The idea of this session

A table, a chart and a list are data, and each needs a level

  • The policy covers all data a public entity receives, produces or manages, in any form
  • An output does not inherit the level of its source
  • Classify when you create it: until someone does, it is treated as Restricted

In the Awn evaluation

One Restricted registry produces a Restricted extract, a Restricted worklist, a Restricted draft table and, after approval, a Public table of referral rates by college.

Analysts classify sources and forget outputs. The slide deck you send to a dean is data; so is the screenshot in a chat. Ask the room what they produced last week that nobody classified.

Sources: Data Classification Policy, scope and principle 3; classification controls.

The starting point

The policy expects you to share, and tells you how

  • Its first principle is a data sharing culture: entities share what they produce
  • The other seven principles are the conditions: legitimate purpose, authorised access, transparency, accountability, security, ethical use, one source of truth
  • For personal data the PDPL's disclosure cases apply on top

The analyst's part

You will be the person who knows what the data contains. You prepare the assessment and the conditions. The data management office decides, with the data protection officer.

The eight principles were taught in FN-106; one line of recap is enough. The attitude matters: a unit that answers every request with "no" teaches partners to stop asking and start finding another way.

Sources: Data Sharing Policy, main principles and roles (clause Eighth, paragraph 6).

The assessment

Three tests decide whether sharing can go ahead

1 · Legitimate purpose

A legal basis or a justified operational need. For the Observatory: its mandate, a public-interest purpose. Met.

2 · Minimum data

Only what the purpose needs. It asked for 11 fields of all 18,000 students. Not met.

3 · The source's consent

Needed for fields the University is not the source of. Met with conditions.

Time. The office assesses within 10 business days and gives its reasons in writing. A rejected request may be corrected and sent again.

The verdicts are the solution of Lab 1.4. A failed test is not the end: the office says what must change, the requester resubmits, and the office decides again within five business days.

Sources: Data Sharing Policy, clause Sixth (paragraph 3) and clause Seventh.

The conditions

Eight headings turn "yes, but" into something signable

  1. Legal basis: the purpose, and no other use
  2. Authorisation: named people, need to know
  3. Data type: the fields, the format, the detail
  4. Pre-processing: masking, aggregation before or after
  1. Sharing means: the channel; retention and destruction
  2. Usage and protection: the level, limits, onward use
  3. Duration and termination: end date, what happens then
  4. Liability: who answers for what
Read the request for its weak points. "By email", "and its contractors", "a consulting firm", "kept for future studies": each one becomes a condition.

These are the eight control groups of the policy's fifth clause. In the lab each heading gets one sentence. Read two of the solution's conditions aloud: the channel (the Government Service Bus or another approved channel, never an attachment) and the destruction of identity numbers after linkage.

Sources: Data Sharing Policy, clause Fifth.
